# 1. Introduction

Urban traffic signal control is a networked decision problem in which local timing decisions propagate through neighboring intersections and may create delayed downstream effects. Multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) has consequently become an important framework for adaptive multi-intersection control, with recent studies emphasizing decentralized communication, graph-based information aggregation, counterfactual credit assignment, and explicit modeling of intersection heterogeneity. These developments improve coordination, but they also expose a fundamental difficulty: more cooperation is not necessarily better. Recent evidence shows that independent control can outperform cooperative MARL in some multi-intersection settings when spatial influence, observation range, or delayed neighbor effects are not handled appropriately.

Most existing cooperative MARL traffic-signal-control methods address this problem inside the learning architecture. Communication mechanisms determine which information is exchanged; centralized critics or counterfactual baselines improve credit assignment; graph models encode spatial dependencies; and Shapley-based rewards can encourage collaboration. These approaches answer how agents should learn to cooperate. A different operational question remains less explored: once a multi-agent controller has already been trained, which intersections should actually be allowed to participate in cooperative intervention?

This distinction matters for deployable traffic control. Enabling every intersection to invoke a learned controller can increase intervention frequency and expose the network to unfavorable cross-intersection interactions. Conversely, a sparse subset of strategically selected intersections may preserve native control elsewhere while retaining most—or even more—of the network benefit. Residual reinforcement learning is particularly suitable for studying this question because the learned policy can operate as a supervisory correction while an established traffic controller remains the default control layer.

We therefore formulate controller participation as a transferable-utility cooperative game. Nine selected traffic signals constitute the player set. For any coalition, only its members are permitted to invoke the validated residual MAPPO supervisor; non-members remain under native actuated control. Holding the learned policy fixed separates coalition valuation from policy retraining. Each coalition is evaluated through paired deterministic SUMO counterfactual replay against the native-actuated baseline of the same traffic realization. Because the player set contains nine intersections, the complete characteristic function can be obtained by enumerating all 2^9=512 coalitions.

The resulting game enables questions that conventional MARL performance comparisons do not directly answer. Exact Shapley values quantify each signal's average marginal contribution across every coalition context. Core feasibility tests whether an efficient allocation can make full cooperation self-enforcing. When the core is empty, least-core and nucleolus analysis quantify and minimize coalition dissatisfaction. This use of cooperative-game stability is conceptually related to transportation cost-allocation studies in ridesharing and collaborative logistics, where the nucleolus is used to control maximum dissatisfaction, but here the characteristic function is generated from counterfactual traffic-control performance rather than monetary transport cost.

The principal contributions are as follows.

1. A coalition-constrained residual-control formulation is developed in which native actuated control remains active for non-members and only coalition members may invoke learned MAPPO interventions.
2. The complete nine-player operational game is evaluated through all 512 coalitions, avoiding sampled or additive approximations of coalition value.
3. Exact Shapley attribution is combined with core, least-core, and nucleolus analysis to distinguish marginal contribution from coalition stability.
4. The experiments show that the highest-value seed-9001 coalition contains five signals and achieves a balanced characteristic value approximately 5.99 times that of the grand coalition while using substantially fewer residual interventions.
5. Utility-weight sensitivity, throughput protection, and four additional held-out seeds are used to test whether the selective-cooperation conclusion depends on a single scalarization or stochastic realization.

The central finding is not that cooperation is undesirable, but that cooperation should be selective. Coalition composition dominates coalition size, and full participation is neither necessary nor consistently preferable in the evaluated network. This shifts the design question from “how can all agents cooperate?” toward “which agents should cooperate under a given operating objective, and how stable is that cooperation?”

# 2. Related Work

## 2.1 Multi-agent reinforcement learning for traffic signal control

MARL addresses the scalability limitations of monolithic network-wide controllers by associating agents with individual intersections or local regions. Recent work has focused heavily on improving coordination under partial observability. Representational-communication approaches learn messages that allow decentralized agents to exchange useful latent information. Graph-based MARL methods exploit road-network topology and diffusion or graph-learning operators to aggregate spatially relevant observations. Other studies explicitly model heterogeneous intersection topology or use multi-level coordination to combine local and network-level objectives. Together, these methods demonstrate that coordination quality depends on how spatial and temporal dependencies are represented, not simply on the number of agents participating.

A particularly relevant recent result questions the assumption that cooperative control must outperform independent control. Ren et al. analyze cases in which independent controllers outperform cooperative MARL and attribute this behavior to observation-zone limitations and time-lagged neighbor effects. Their explicit-neighborhood-backtracking framework improves how agents account for delayed upstream/downstream influence. The present study addresses a complementary question. Rather than redesigning the communication mechanism to make universal cooperation more effective, it holds a trained residual policy fixed and evaluates whether universal participation should occur at all.

## 2.2 Credit assignment, counterfactual reasoning, and Shapley-based cooperation

Credit assignment is a central difficulty in cooperative MARL because network-level performance emerges from interacting local decisions. Counterfactual actor-critic approaches address this by comparing an agent's realized action with counterfactual alternatives while conditioning on the actions of other agents. In traffic signal control, Song et al. use a counterfactual multi-agent actor-critic framework with a scheduler to improve collaboration and credit assignment across multiple intersections.

Cooperative-game concepts have also entered MARL-TSC through Shapley-informed rewards. Liu et al. construct a Shapley-value reward to estimate an agent's contribution within a cooperative neighborhood and use it during learning to encourage collaboration. More recently, Shapley-based congestion attribution has been used to identify high-contribution intersections and guide Top-k joint training or decision ordering. These studies demonstrate the usefulness of marginal-contribution reasoning, but their Shapley analysis primarily supports reward construction, attribution, or training efficiency.

Our use of the Shapley value differs in both timing and purpose. Shapley values are computed after training from the complete operational characteristic function generated by actual coalition-constrained replay. They therefore describe the marginal value of permitting an intersection to participate in residual control across every possible coalition context. Moreover, Shapley attribution is not treated as a stability guarantee: it is evaluated alongside the core and nucleolus.

## 2.3 Residual and constrained reinforcement learning for signal control

Traffic signal control is safety- and operations-critical, making unconstrained replacement of established controller logic difficult to justify. Residual reinforcement learning offers an intermediate architecture in which a learned component modifies or supplements a baseline controller rather than replacing it completely. Prior traffic-control research has used residual RL to improve stability, and SafeLight demonstrates a safety-enhanced residual formulation that integrates domain constraints into RL-based signal control.

The present framework adopts the same broad deployment principle but uses residual control for a different experimental purpose. Native actuated logic remains the default at every intersection, and coalition membership determines only whether the learned supervisor may request a limited green extension. Consequently, coalition value reflects the incremental benefit of selective learned intervention over an operational baseline rather than the performance of an entirely substituted controller.

## 2.4 Cooperative-game stability and the nucleolus in transportation

Cooperative game theory distinguishes contribution attribution from coalition stability. The Shapley value averages marginal contributions over coalition orders, whereas the core asks whether any coalition can improve upon an efficient grand-coalition allocation. When the core is empty, least-core and nucleolus concepts quantify unavoidable dissatisfaction and seek allocations that progressively minimize the largest coalition excesses.

Transportation research has used these concepts mainly for allocation problems. Lu and Quadrifoglio formulate ridesharing cost allocation as a cooperative game and develop nucleolus-based procedures aimed at minimizing maximum participant dissatisfaction. Öner and Kuyzu similarly study core stability and nucleolus-based cost allocation in collaborative truckload transportation. These studies establish the relevance of the nucleolus for transportation cooperation, but the players typically represent customers, shippers, or service participants and the characteristic function represents monetary or routing cost.

In contrast, the present game treats traffic signals as operational players and constructs coalition value from simulation-based network-control outcomes. This allows the core and nucleolus to answer whether the performance benefit created by a learned traffic-control coalition is compatible with stable participation. To our knowledge, the combination of fixed-policy coalition-constrained counterfactual replay with complete Shapley, core, and nucleolus analysis has received limited attention in MARL traffic signal control.

## 2.5 Positioning of the present study

The literature can therefore be separated into three complementary questions: how intersections should exchange information and learn jointly; how contribution should be attributed during cooperative learning; and how benefits or costs should be allocated among participants in a cooperative game. This study connects these strands at the post-training operational level. It does not propose Shapley values as another reward function and does not assume that the grand coalition is the desired endpoint. Instead, it constructs the exact coalition landscape of a validated residual MARL controller, identifies beneficial subsets, and evaluates whether the resulting cooperative structure is stable.

This positioning is particularly important because the exact results reveal strong non-additivity. An intersection that is detrimental alone may become useful in combination, while individually beneficial intersections may interact antagonistically. Such effects cannot be resolved from singleton ranking or local credit assignment alone and motivate complete coalition valuation when the number of controlled intersections makes enumeration computationally feasible.
